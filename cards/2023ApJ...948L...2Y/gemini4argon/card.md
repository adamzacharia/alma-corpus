## Analysis recipe

1. **Self-calibrate and image the Band 7 dust continuum**: Combine the four continuum basebands while excluding channels with potential line emission, perform two iterations of phase-only self-calibration on Stokes I, and clean using CASA tclean with the Hogbom algorithm and Briggs weighting  
  > "To construct them we combined the four continuum basebands avoiding some spectral channels with potential line emission (C$^{17}$O (3-2) and several CH$_3$OH transitions). We ran two phase-only self-calibration iterations on the continuum Stokes I data." (Observations); "To clean the images, we use the CASA task `tclean` using the Hogbom algorithm with a Briggs weighting of 0.5." (Observations)
   - software: CASA, tclean
   - parameter: Self-calibration solution intervals: infinite (1st iteration) and 25 seconds (2nd iteration)  
  > "The solution intervals used for the first and second were infinite (i.e., a solution interval over the whole dataset) and 25 seconds, respectively." (Observations)
   - parameter: Briggs robust = 0.5, Hogbom algorithm  
  > "To clean the images, we use the CASA task `tclean` using the Hogbom algorithm with a Briggs weighting of 0.5." (Observations)
   - fitting: _not stated_
   - validation: Compared measured peak positions before and after self-calibration to verify positional accuracy was preserved  
  > "Note that, in this case, the self-calibration did not affect the positional accuracy. The difference between the measured peak positions before and after self-calibration are well within astrometric accuracy." (Observations)
2. **Determine the stellar center at the epoch of observation**: Propagate the Gaia DR3 2016 stellar coordinates to the 2021.5 observation epoch using Gaia DR3 proper motions  
  > "According the Gaia DR3, in the year 2016, the star is at ($16^{\rm h}27^{\rm m}37\mbox{$^{\rm s}\mskip-7.6mu.\,$}180$,$-24^{\circ}30'35\mbox{$''\mskip-7.6mu.\,$}416$). It has an proper motion of $-8.72\rm\, mas/yr$ in RA and $-24.4\rm\, mas/yr$ in Dec. We derived the location at the time of our observation as ($16^{\rm h}27^{\rm m}37\mbox{$^{\rm s}\mskip-7.6mu.\,$}177$,$-24^{\circ}30'35\mbox{$''\mskip-7.6mu.\,$}550$), assuming $5.5$ years time difference." (Observations)
   - parameter: Time difference = 5.5 years  
  > "assuming $5.5$ years time difference." (Observations)
   - assumption: Distance = 136 pc from Gaia DR3 parallax  
  > "From the parallax, we derive a distance of $136$ pc" (Observations)
   - fitting: _not stated_
   - validation: Spatial coincidence within 3 mas between the Gaia-inferred star position and the 2D-Gaussian center of the central submillimeter source  
  > "The center is only $3\rm\,mas$ west of the central star, well within one pixel of our image ($20$ mas). The spatial coincidence between the star location inferred from the Gaia data and the center of the central emission adds confidence to the location determination to both of them." (Detection of central emission)
3. **Deproject continuum image and fit elliptical ring**: Deproject the continuum image to the disk plane and fit an ellipse parameterized by semi-major axis, eccentricity, and major-axis position angle (First with focus fixed at the star, then with free focus location) by minimizing a custom asymmetric loss function  
  > "Assuming an inclination angle of $50^\circ$ and a position angle of $100^\circ$ (cite: Bruderer2014), we deproject the image to the disk plane." (Eccentric ring); "To fit the elliptical ring, we first parameterize the ellipse with the semi-major axis $a$, the eccentricity $e$, and the position angle of the major axis of the ellipse. We fix one of the foci on the central star. The fitting is done on the deprojected image, and the loss function is defined as $\mathcal{L}\equiv \sum I(x,y) f(dr(x,y))$" (Eccentric ring); "To explore these possibilities, we relax the constraint in Sec. 3.1 and introduce the location of the focus as two additional parameters." (Secondary stellar companion?)
   - parameter: delta_p = 0.02'', asymmetric loss weighting factor 1.5 for dr > 0  
  > "where $\delta_p=0.02\ensuremath{''}$ is about the pixel size of our image, or $1/5$ of the beam size." (Eccentric ring)
   - assumption: Inclination angle = 50 deg, position angle = 100 deg  
  > "Assuming an inclination angle of $50^\circ$ and a position angle of $100^\circ$ (cite: Bruderer2014), we deproject the image to the disk plane." (Eccentric ring)
   - assumption: One focus fixed on the central star (in the baseline fit)  
  > "We fix one of the foci on the central star." (Eccentric ring)
   - fitting: Minimization of custom loss function L = sum I(x,y) f(dr(x,y))  
  > "The fitting is done on the deprojected image, and the loss function is defined as $\mathcal{L}\equiv \sum I(x,y) f(dr(x,y))$, where $I(x,y)$ is the intensity and $dr(x,y)$ is the difference of the radial distance from the center between the point in the image and the target ellipse." (Eccentric ring)
   - validation: Tested sensitivity to inclination angle and relaxed the focus constraint to examine 1000 orbits within 1% of the minimum loss function  
  > "So the eccentricity does not depend on the adopted inclination angle too much." (Eccentric ring); "In Fig. 4, we plot $1000$ orbits and their foci with reasonable fits to our data. Among these ellipses, the largest loss function is only $\sim1\%$ larger than the smallest one." (Secondary stellar companion?)
4. **Fit central source and estimate inner disk dust mass**: Fit a 2D Gaussian to the central continuum emission, evaluate stellar photosphere and ionized wind contributions, and calculate optically thin thermal dust mass from the 3-sigma contour mean flux density and area  
  > "We conduct a simple 2D-Gaussian fit to this central continuum emission." (Detection of central emission); "The mean flux density within the $3\sigma$ contour is about $8.65\times 10^{-5}\rm\, Jy/beam$, corresponding to a brightness temperature of $0.164$ K. This is extremely optically thin for any reasonable dust temperature. The area of this region is measured as $291\rm\, au^2$." (Detection of central emission)
   - parameter: Area within 3-sigma contour = 291 au^2; mean flux density = 8.65e-5 Jy/beam  
  > "The mean flux density within the $3\sigma$ contour is about $8.65\times 10^{-5}\rm\, Jy/beam$, corresponding to a brightness temperature of $0.164$ K. This is extremely optically thin for any reasonable dust temperature. The area of this region is measured as $291\rm\, au^2$." (Detection of central emission)
   - assumption: Dust temperature = 100 K  
  > "Given the small size of the central source (maximum distance from the central star is $\sim 10$ au), we assume a dust temperature of $100$ K." (Detection of central emission)
   - assumption: Dust opacity = 3.5 cm^2/g at 870 um  
  > "We also assume a dust opacity of $3.5\rm\, cm^2/g$, which is the fiducial dust model from (cite: Birnstiel2018) with $1$ mm maximum grain size at $870\rm\, \mu m$ wavelength." (Detection of central emission)
   - assumption: Optically thin thermal dust emission  
  > "This is extremely optically thin for any reasonable dust temperature." (Detection of central emission)
   - fitting: 2D-Gaussian fit to the central emission  
  > "We conduct a simple 2D-Gaussian fit to this central continuum emission." (Detection of central emission)
   - validation: Calculated stellar blackbody flux (7.22e-6 Jy) and optically thin thermal jet free-free emission (29 uJy) to confirm neither can explain the measured flux  
  > "We can then calculate the flux density at the wavelength of $870\rm\, \mu m$ as $7.22\times 10^{-6}\rm\, Jy$. This is $21$ times smaller than what we detect." (Detection of central emission); "Assuming that the emission of a thermal jet is optically thin from 34 to 343.5 GHz, the free-free contribution would be $29 \mu$Jy at 343.5 GHz, roughly 10 times smaller than the measured flux." (Detection of central emission)
5. **Measure multi-epoch proper motion of the crescent peak**: Use CASA imfit to fit a 2D Gaussian to the crescent across 5 ALMA epochs spanning 9 years (2012-2021) and perform a linear fit to the peak positions after subtracting the Gaia stellar proper motion  
  > "The 9 year separation in observing time allows us to constrain the proper motion of the crescent-shaped structure. We obtained the archival ALMA data and use `CASA` `imfit` to conduct 2D Gaussian fit to the crescent." (Proper motion); "With these caveats in mind, we fit the proper motion of the peak after subtracting the stellar proper motion as $(-3.0\pm1.9, 3.0\pm 2.5)\rm\, mas/yr$." (Proper motion)
   - software: CASA, imfit
   - parameter: 5 epochs (2012.50, 2014.45, 2015.58, 2018.63, 2021.50); epoch uncertainty = 0.1 yr; astrometric error doubled for resolution < 0.15''  
  > "For observations with resolution finer than $0.15\ensuremath{''}$, the positional error can be up to a factor of two higher. Only our observations are this high resolution, so we doubled the aforementioned astrometric error to be conservative." (Proper motion); "We also put $0.1$ error for all epoches, which is on the order of one month." (Proper motion)
   - assumption: Keplerian velocity v_K = 5.438 km/s at r = 60 au; distance = 136 pc; negligible azimuthal offset between Band 9 and Band 7 peaks; Gaia astrometric error ignored  
  > "We do not consider the astrometric error from Gaia." (Proper motion); "Since the peak at $440\rm\, \mu m$ was reported to coincide with that at 9 mm (cite: vanderMarel2015), it is reasonable to ignore the potential azimuthal displacement here" (Proper motion); "where as $v_K=5.438\rm\, km/s$ is the Keplerian velocity at the distance of $60$ au, the location of the peak." (Proper motion)
   - fitting: 2D Gaussian fit in CASA imfit per epoch, followed by linear proper motion fit in RA and Dec  
  > "We obtained the archival ALMA data and use `CASA` `imfit` to conduct 2D Gaussian fit to the crescent." (Proper motion); "With these caveats in mind, we fit the proper motion of the peak after subtracting the stellar proper motion as $(-3.0\pm1.9, 3.0\pm 2.5)\rm\, mas/yr$." (Proper motion)
   - validation: Compared multi-epoch peak trajectory against both circular and eccentric orbit predictions  
  > "In panel *(e)* of Fig. 3, we over plot the circular orbit and the best-fit elliptical orbit on the image." (Proper motion)
6. **Model line-of-sight velocity map of the eccentric orbit**: Compute the line-of-sight velocity map and iso-velocity contours for the best-fit e = 0.27 elliptical orbit and compare with a circular Keplerian rotation model  
  > "The velocity map with an eccentricity of $e=0.27$ from our best-fit model is shown in Fig. 5. We adopt a systemic velocity of 4.55 km/s as in (cite: vanderMarel2021_IRS48) and the resulting velocity map is similar to their Fig. 1." (Velocity maps of line emissions); "In order to compare with Keplerian rotation, we also plot the iso-velocity contours for the elliptical orbit as black solid lines and the contours with same levels for Keplerian rotation as black dashed lines." (Velocity maps of line emissions)
   - parameter: Eccentricity e = 0.27, systemic velocity = 4.55 km/s  
  > "The velocity map with an eccentricity of $e=0.27$ from our best-fit model is shown in Fig. 5. We adopt a systemic velocity of 4.55 km/s as in (cite: vanderMarel2021_IRS48)" (Velocity maps of line emissions)
   - assumption: Gas is co-moving with the dust  
  > "The elliptical orbit also has an impact on the velocity maps of line emission, assuming the gas is co-moving with the dust." (Velocity maps of line emissions)
   - fitting: _not stated_
   - validation: Qualitatively compared the predicted red-shifted/blue-shifted contour asymmetry with published H2CO and 13CO velocity maps  
  > "This asymmetry that the red-shifted contours are larger than their blue-shifted counterparts in velocity maps is visible in both $\rm H_2CO$ and $\rm ^{13}CO$ (cite: vanderMarel2014; vanderMarel2021_IRS48), which lends support to the elliptical orbit" (Velocity maps of line emissions)

## Qualitative findings

- A long tail of dust emission trailing behind the crescent and diffuse emission in the northwest form a complete elliptical ring traced along 95% of its circumference.  
  > "In addition to the well-known crescent-shaped structure, we also detect a long tail of dust emission trailing behind the crescent-shaped structure, with respect to the counter-clockwise rotation of the disk (cite: Bruderer2014), and some diffuse emission with over $3\sigma$ detection in the north west part of the disk." (Results); "For the first time we are able to significantly trace and detect emission from about $95\%$ of the ring crossing the crescent-shaped structure." (Eccentric ring)
- The azimuthal flux density profile along the fitted ellipse is asymmetric and resembles a droplet or tadpole, with a round ~90 deg head and a ~180 deg tail.  
  > "We can see that the density structure is not symmetric with respect to the peak, and it resembles a droplet or a tadpole. The head is rounder with about $90^\circ$ degree spread in azimuthal angle whereas the tail spreads over almost $180^\circ$." (Eccentric ring)
- Compact unresolved emission is detected at the 15-sigma level coincident with the central star (within 3 mas), consistent with an inner disk of radius at most a couple of au.  
  > "This source has a peak flux density of $0.21\rm\, mJy/beam$, at a signal-to-noise level $15\sigma$." (Detection of central emission); "If the central object is an unresolved inner disk, the disk radius is a couple of astronomical units at most." (Summary)
- Multi-epoch peak positions of the crescent do not follow either a simple circular orbit or the fitted eccentric orbit.  
  > "We can see that the data points do not follow either a circular orbit or an eccentric orbit." (Proper motion)
- An e = 0.27 elliptical orbit produces larger red-shifted iso-velocity contours than blue-shifted contours, qualitatively matching observed asymmetries in H2CO and 13CO velocity maps.  
  > "We can see that the red region is significantly larger than the blue region, and the red-shifted solid contours are larger than their blue-shifted counterparts." (Velocity maps of line emissions); "This asymmetry that the red-shifted contours are larger than their blue-shifted counterparts in velocity maps is visible in both $\rm H_2CO$ and $\rm ^{13}CO$ (cite: vanderMarel2014; vanderMarel2021_IRS48), which lends support to the elliptical orbit" (Velocity maps of line emissions)

## Reasoning

- Why this data: Deep, high-resolution Band 7 observations (originally designed for dust polarization) provide the sensitivity (14 uJy/beam) and resolution (0.11'' x 0.072'') needed to trace faint dust emission around the full ring and resolve the inner cavity emission from the outer crescent.  
  > "The experiment was primarily designed for studying the polarization of the dust emission toward IRS 48. Hence, we tuned four ALMA basebands dedicated for the dust continuum emission" (Observations); "Our ALMA observations are the deepest high resolution (sub)mm observations towards IRS 48 to date, reaching a noise level of $14\rm\, \mu Jy/beam$ at an angular resolution of $0.11\ensuremath{''}\times 0.072\ensuremath{''}$. For the first time we are able to significantly trace and detect emission from about $95\%$ of the ring crossing the crescent-shaped structure." (Eccentric ring)
- Why this method: An asymmetric radial loss function was engineered for the ellipse fit to force the fitted ellipse to pass through the central peak of the crescent-shaped structure.  
  > "The loss function is engineered to have our fitted ellipse crossing the central peak of the crescent-shaped structure, as the one we present in the next paragraph." (Eccentric ring)
- Alternative: Circular orbit for the dust ring — rejected because the northern part of the ring is ~1.8 times further from the central star than the southern crescent peak.  
  > "We can see that the north-northwest tail behind the crescent-shaped structure and the diffuse emission in the northwest part of the disk structure form an elliptical pattern rather than a circular pattern." (Eccentric ring); "The necessity for the ring to be eccentric is clear given that the north part is $\sim 1.8$ times further from the central star than the peak" (Eccentric ring)
- Alternative: Protostellar photosphere emission as the origin of the central source — rejected because the expected 870 um photospheric flux is 7.22e-6 Jy, which is 21 times smaller than detected.  
  > "Firstly, the central emission cannot be completely explained by emission coming from a central star." (Detection of central emission); "We can then calculate the flux density at the wavelength of $870\rm\, \mu m$ as $7.22\times 10^{-6}\rm\, Jy$. This is $21$ times smaller than what we detect." (Detection of central emission)
- Alternative: Optically thin thermal free-free emission from an ionized wind/jet as the origin of the central source — rejected because extrapolating the 34 GHz flux (36 uJy) with spectral index -0.1 gives 29 uJy at 343.5 GHz, roughly 10 times smaller than measured.  
  > "Assuming that the emission of a thermal jet is optically thin from 34 to 343.5 GHz, the free-free contribution would be $29 \mu$Jy at 343.5 GHz, roughly 10 times smaller than the measured flux." (Detection of central emission); "Neither the emission from the protostellar photosphere, nor the emission from an ionized wind could alone (or added) explain the measured flux from the central source in the ALMA image." (Detection of central emission)
- Alternative: Non-thermal magnetospheric synchrotron emission, ionized emission from the dust sublimation wall, or pebble/planetesimal collisions — set aside in favor of the simpler thermal inner disk hypothesis.  
  > "Still other possible contributions can be due to the non-thermal synchrotron emission from the protostellar magnetosphere (cite: Andre1997), the ionized emission from the dust sublimation wall (cite: 2020AnezLopez), or the collisions from pebbles and planetesimals that heat the dust of a disk (cite: 2020Matra). The former two hypotheses are expected to be more prominent in the centimeter wavelength range, while the third hypothesis is somehow a more exotic solution that may need more theoretical work to be supported." (Detection of central emission)

- Limitation (authors): The emission along ~5% of the ellipse is not robustly detected, so only a lower limit (>=400) can be placed on the maximum-to-minimum intensity ratio.  
  > "Since the emission along part of the ellipse is not robustly detected, the ratio of the maximum intensity is at least 400 times the minimum." (Eccentric ring)
- Limitation (authors): The central source is not spatially resolved by the 0.1'' beam, so its radius is only constrained as an upper limit.  
  > "We do not resolve the central emission with $0.1''$ beam. If the central object is an unresolved inner disk, the disk radius is a couple of astronomical units at most." (Summary)
- Limitation (authors): The proper motion analysis combines Band 9 (440 um) and Band 7 (870 um) observations and ignores Gaia astrometric errors and potential primary star reflex motion due to an unseen companion.  
  > "We do not consider the astrometric error from Gaia." (Proper motion); "We note that the first two points, from (cite: vanderMarel2013) and (cite: vanderMarel2015), are observed at Band 9 ($440\rm\, \mu m$), whereas the others are observed at Band 7 ($870\rm\, \mu m$)." (Proper motion); "in the case of an undetected stellar companion with a non-negligible mass (as suggested by (cite: Calcino2019)), the Gaia proper motions do not take into account the possible orbital motion of the primary star." (Proper motion)
- Limitation (authors): When the ellipse focus is allowed to vary freely, the dispersed nature of the dust emission leaves the focus location degenerate over ~50 au.  
  > "Despite the similarity in their loss functions, the location of the foci differs by almost $50$ au." (Secondary stellar companion?); "The uncertainty of the fitted focus mostly comes from the dispersed nature of the emission. Deeper observations with lower noise are unlikely to give much better constraints." (Secondary stellar companion?)
- Limitation (authors): Quantitative consistency between the gas velocity map and the e = 0.27 dust ring eccentricity has not yet been established.  
  > "although it remains to be determined whether the velocity map is quantitatively consistent with the eccentricity of $e=0.27$ inferred from the dust emission distribution." (Velocity maps of line emissions)
- Caveat (inferred, ours): Fitting a 2D Gaussian (via CASA imfit) to a strongly asymmetric, crescent-shaped structure across epochs with very different beam sizes (from 0.49''x0.39'' to 0.11''x0.072'') can introduce systematic resolution-dependent shifts in the fitted centroid compared to the true peak.
- Caveat (inferred, ours): The custom loss function L = sum I(x,y) f(dr) weights pixels linearly by intensity I(x,y); because the crescent peak has an intensity contrast of ~270-400 relative to the faint northern ring, the fit is heavily dominated by the bright crescent unless an explicit mask or log-scaling is applied.
- Caveat (inferred, ours): Phase-only self-calibration was performed on the 2021 data; although the authors checked that peak positions before and after self-calibration agreed, any initial phase-center offset prior to self-calibration directly enters the 2021 epoch astrometry.
