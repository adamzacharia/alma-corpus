2. **Calibrate, image, and measure 8.8 mm ATCA continuum fluxes**: Calibrate ATCA visibilities with mfcal and gpcal, flag noisy data with uvflag and blflag, image at 34 GHz with robust=2 weighting and clean to 5 sigma, and measure unresolved point-source flux density with imfit.  
  > "After calibrating the data, images at 34 GHz were produced using robust weighting of 2 to achieve natural weighting and retain maximum detectable flux.    - parameter: robust weighting = 2, clean threshold = 5 sigma, imfit source = 'point', path length noise flagging threshold = 400 um, amplitude deviation flagging = 10%  
  > "we flagged all data with a seeing monitor RMS path length noise above 400 $\rm \mu$m using *uvflag*, and calibrator amplitude readings that deviated more than 10% from the mean flux using *blflag*."   > "We adopt a 10% uncertainty on the absolute flux scale for our ATCA observations that is typical at these wavelengths (cite: 2012MNRAS.425.3137U), and in agreement with the variations we observe of the gain calibrator flux." (ATCA Observations and Data Reduction)

3. **Fit Spectral Energy Distributions (SEDs) to derive dust temperatures**: Simultaneously fit PHOENIX stellar photosphere models and one or two modified blackbody disc components to multi-wavelength photometry.  
  > "We then simultaneously fit stellar and disc components as described by (cite: 2019MNRAS.488.3588Y); we use PHOENIX models (cite: 2012IAUS..282..235A) for the stellar photosphere component, and a ‘modified’ blackbody function for the disc." (Spectral Energy Distributions)

   - fitting: Simultaneous stellar and modified blackbody fit as described by Yelverton et al. 2019  
  > "We then simultaneously fit stellar and disc components as described by (cite: 2019MNRAS.488.3588Y); we use PHOENIX models (cite: 2012IAUS..282..235A) for the stellar photosphere component, and a ‘modified’ blackbody function for the disc." (Spectral Energy Distributions)
   - validation: _not stated_
4. **Calculate grain size distribution power-law index q and perform statistical tests**: Calculate Planck slope alpha_Pl and mm spectral index alpha_mm between 8.8 mm and 1.3 mm, derive q = (alpha_mm - alpha_Pl)/beta_s + 3, and run Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Pearson correlation, and Fisher's exact tests across the 22-disc sample.  
  > "The mm spectral index can then be calculated via $\alpha_{\rm mm} = |\log(F_{\nu_1}/F_{\nu_2}) / \log(\nu_1/\nu_2)|$. Assuming both flux measurements are in the Rayleigh-Jeans limit, $q$ is then derived through the relation: $$\begin{equation} \label{eqn:q} q = \frac{\rm \alpha_{mm}-\rm \alpha_{Pl}}{\beta_{\rm s}}+3 \end{equation}$$" (Determining the Grain Size Distribution Parameter); "Using the SCIPY.STATS.PEARSONR function, we find a moderate correlation between $q$ and the interpolated flux with a Pearson coefficient of 0.57." (Discussion); "To check this, we use a Fisher’s exact test on our data from Figure [4](#fig:qvsflux)b (a Fisher’s test exactly calculates the significance of there existing two distinct distributions in a sample)." (Discussion)
   - software: Python, SciPy
   - parameter: beta_s = 1.8  
  > "We therefore assume that our grains are composed of astronomical silicate where $\beta_{\rm s} = 1.8$, consistent with many other debris disc studies (cite: 2016ApJ...823...79M; 2017MNRAS.468.2719M)." (Determining the Grain Size Distribution Parameter)

## Qualitative findings

- The four newly observed debris discs all have low grain size distribution parameters (q < 3.2), suggesting colliding bodies have lower tensile strength and clumping is dominated by self-gravity.  
  > "We present the longest wavelength observations to date of HD 48370, CPD-72 2713, HD 131488, and HD 32297 at 8.8 mm, and find that the $q$ value of these sources are all quite low ($q < 3.2$), suggesting that the colliding bodies in these discs have a lower tensile strength and clumping is dominated by self-gravity." (Conclusion)
- There may exist two distributions of q in the debris disc sample: a broad distribution (q ~ 3.2-3.7) for typical gas-poor discs and a narrow distribution (q < 3.2) for bright gas-rich discs, or alternatively an observational bias against detecting faint discs with steep size distributions at cm wavelengths.  
  > "We suggest the possibility of two distributions of $q$ in our debris disc sample; a broad distribution (where $q \sim 3.2-3.7$) for "typical" debris discs (gas-poor/non-detection), and a narrow distribution (where $q < 3.2$) for bright gas-rich discs." (Abstract); "Or alternatively, we suggest that there exists an observational bias between the grain size distribution parameter and absolute flux which may be attributed to the detection rates of faint debris discs at $\rm \sim$cm wavelengths." (Abstract)

## Reasoning

- Why this data: Observing at ~1 cm (8.8 mm) provides a long lever arm with 1.3 mm ALMA data to constrain the mm spectral index alpha_mm and is effectively in the Rayleigh-Jeans regime for typical disk temperatures.  
  > "Despite their faint emission, observing at longer wavelengths ($\rm \sim$1 cm) provides a better constraint on the mm spectral index (a long lever arm) and is effectively in the Rayleigh-Jeans regime for typical disk temperatures." (Introduction); "The PLATYPUS sample are selected to have (1) declinations below 20 degrees in order to be observable with the ATCA array, (2) complementary ALMA observations at 1.3 mm, and (3) are relatively compact to maximize surface brightness sensitivity." (The Sample)

- Alternative: Two-component vs single-component modified blackbody SED fits: two disc components are used only where a significantly better fit is found (HD 131488 and HD 32297).

- Alternative: Observational bias vs two intrinsic populations of q: the correlation between q and flux could be an observational sensitivity bias against faint discs with steep spectral slopes rather than two distinct physical distributions.  
  > "Or alternately given that our systems are faint in apparent flux, it is possible that an observational bias exists, especially since the Pearson coefficient becomes weaker once we scale the flux with the distance." (Discussion)
