## Analysis recipe

1. **Calibrate and image EVN 5 GHz and archival HSA 1.4 GHz VLBI visibility data**: Phase-referencing calibration in AIPS (called via ParselTongue for EVN) including fringe fitting, ionospheric and parallactic angle corrections, bandpass calibration, and calibrator self-calibration, followed by deconvolution in DIFMAP.  
  > "All the related AIPS tasks were called via the PARSELTONGUE interface (cite: Kettenis2006) and integrated in a single script." (The EVN experiment at 5 GHz); "The deconvolution was performed in DIFMAP (cite: Shepherd1994)." (The EVN experiment at 5 GHz); "We reduced the data following the VLBA continuum data calibration method recommended by the AIPS cookbook in Appendix A." (The HSA data at 1.4 GHz)
   - software: AIPS, PARSELTONGUE, DIFMAP
   - parameter: Fringe-fitting solution interval ~1 min, median filter smoothing width 0.5 h on fringe rate  
  > "Sixth, the fringe-fitting on the calibrator data was performed with a solution interval of $\sim$1 min." (The EVN experiment at 5 GHz); "To gain accurate phase interpolations between scans, we smoothed the fringe rate solution using a median filter with a width of half hour." (The EVN experiment at 5 GHz)
   - parameter: Arecibo (AR) excluded in final HSA imaging due to ~100 deg residual phase errors  
  > "We noticed that there were significant residual phase errors ($\sim$100$\ensuremath{^\circ}$) on the baselines to `AR` in the dirty map. Because of this issue, the data of `AR` were excluded in the final imaging results." (The HSA data at 1.4 GHz)
   - fitting: _not stated_
   - validation: Checked dirty map for residual phase errors on baselines to Arecibo and verified calibrator positions against ICRF3 and Gaia.  
  > "We noticed that there were significant residual phase errors ($\sim$100$\ensuremath{^\circ}$) on the baselines to `AR` in the dirty map." (The HSA data at 1.4 GHz); "The separations between the and ICRF3 positions are small, 1.2 mas for J1220$+$3431 and 0.2 mas for J1220$+$3343." (Location of the accreting IMBH)
2. **Calibrate and image archival Jansky VLA 12–18 GHz and ALMA 237 GHz continuum data**: Process VLA data with the VLA CASA pipeline; calibrate ALMA extended and compact configurations separately with the ALMA CASA pipeline, concatenate calibrated visibilities, and image line-free channels for individual epochs and combined data.  
  > "The data reduction was performed using the Common Astronomy Software Applications package (cite: McMullin2007). With the VLA pipeline, the data were calibrated and processed." (The Jansky VLA data at 12–18 GHz); "The data corresponding to each of the configurations were calibrated separately by the ALMA pipeline using CASA versions 5.1.1-5 (extended-configuration) and 5.4.0-68 (compact-configuration). The calibrated data from both configurations were concatenated. For each of the configurations, as well as for the combined data, line-free channels were used to create continuum images." (The ALMA data at 237 GHz)
   - software: CASA
   - parameter: Aggregate continuum bandwidth 6.9 GHz centred around 237.1 GHz  
  > "The resulting total aggregate bandwidth is 6.9 GHz, centred around 237.1 GHz." (The ALMA data at 237 GHz)
   - assumption: Assumed flux and spectral index at 230.3 GHz for J1229+0203: 4.8 Jy (alpha=-0.83) on 2018 Mar 22, 6.0 Jy (alpha=-0.75) on 2018 Aug 28, 3.6 Jy (alpha=-0.91) on 2019 Jan 23  
  > "2018 Mar 22 | extended configuration | 15–784 | 4.8 Jy; $\alpha$=-0.83 | 373$\pm$3 mJy | 18 | 46" (Table 3); "2018 Aug 28 | extended configuration | 15–782 | 6.0 Jy; $\alpha$=-0.75 | 433$\pm$5 mJy | 18 | 44" (Table 3); "2019 Jan 23 | compact configuration | 15–314 | 3.6 Jy; $\alpha$=-0.91 | 253$\pm$2 mJy | 7 | 48" (Table 3)
   - fitting: _not stated_
   - validation: Confirmed VLA imaging results against previously published results by Saikia et al. (2018).  
  > "With the VLA observations at 12–18 GHz, (cite: Saikia2018) revealed an elongated structure in the radio nucleus of NGC 4395. Our independent data reduction and imaging analysis have fully confirmed those imaging results." (Multi-frequency imaging results)
3. **Fit source models to components E and C to measure positions, sizes, flux densities, and in-band spectral indices**: Fit each discrete feature starting with a point-source model, progressing to circular or elliptical Gaussian models if ~5 sigma residual peaks remained, and include in-band spectral index for broad-band VLA data.  
  > "To characterise these components, we also fit the data to point-source and Gaussian models. During the fitting process, we started to fit each relatively discrete image feature to a simple point-source model. If the point-source model gave some $\ensuremath{\lesssim}5\sigma$ noise peaks in the residual map, we would fit the feature to the more complex circular or elliptical Gaussian model." (Multi-frequency imaging results); "In case of the broad-band VLA observations, we also included the spectral index in the models." (Multi-frequency imaging results)
   - parameter: 5 per cent systematic flux error added in quadrature to formal error for HSA 1.4 GHz flux density  
  > "With an elliptical Gaussian model (E1) and a point source model (E2), we recover a total flux density of $0.72 \pm 0.05$ mJy, where the error is the quadratic sum of a 5 per cent systematic error and the formal error." (Multi-frequency imaging results)
   - fitting: Formal uncertainties estimated via normalising the reduced chi^2 to unity.  
  > "The formal uncertainties are estimated via normalising the reduced $\chi^2$ to unity." (Multi-frequency imaging results)
   - validation: Inspected residual maps for noise peaks and compared 2008 HSA model-fitting results with 2005 HSA results from Wrobel & Ho (2006).  
  > "All these results are consistent with the HSA imaging results reported previously by (cite: Wrobel2006) and further confirms that it is a non-thermal component with an average brightness temperature of $T_{\rm b}=(2.3\pm0.4) \times10^6$ K at 1.4 GHz." (Multi-frequency imaging results)
4. **Derive multi-frequency radio spectra, brightness temperatures, luminosity limits, and physical scale of the torus**: Combine HSA, EVN, VLA, ALMA, and literature flux densities to fit power-law spectra S(nu) proportional to nu^alpha for components E and C, compute brightness temperatures and 5-sigma radio luminosity upper limits at the Gaia IMBH position, and compare component C size with the IMBH gravitational sphere of influence.  
  > "We define the spectral index $\alpha$ with the power-law spectrum $S(\nu) \propto \nu^{\alpha}$." (Introduction); "The broad-band radio spectrum can be described by a single power law function. The best-fitting model for the non-simultaneous spectrum between 1.4 and 237 GHz is $S_{\nu} = (0.32\pm0.08) \nu^{-0.12\pm0.08}$." (Nature of the components E and C); "If there is a compact jet base formed by the central accreting IMBH of NGC 4395, the HSA and EVN non-detections at the *Gaia* position allow us to set the 5$\sigma$ upper limits on its luminosity, $L_{\rm R} = \nu L_{\nu} = 1.3\times10^{33}$ erg s$^{-1}$ at 1.4 GHz and $L_{\rm R} = 4.7\times10^{33}$ erg s$^{-1}$ at 5.0 GHz." (Implications)
   - assumption: Standard LambdaCDM cosmology with H0 = 71 km s^-1 Mpc^-1, Omega_m = 0.27, Omega_Lambda = 0.73, giving luminosity distance 4.5 Mpc (scale ~0.021 pc mas^-1 at 4.3 Mpc)  
  > "Throughout the paper, a standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model with H$_\mathrm{0}$ = 71 km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$, $\Omega_\mathrm{m}$ = 0.27, $\Omega_{\Lambda}$ = 0.73 is adopted. For our target, this gives a luminosity distance of 4.5 Mpc, fully in agreement with the Cepheid distance $4.3\pm0.3$ Mpc presented by (cite: Thim2004)." (Introduction)
   - assumption: No significant time variability when fitting non-simultaneous multi-frequency spectra  
  > "Assuming no significant time variability, we can derive a spectral index of $\alpha=-0.64\pm0.05$ for the component E in Fig. 4." (Multi-frequency imaging results)
   - fitting: Single power-law fit to non-simultaneous multi-frequency flux densities.  
  > "The broad-band radio spectrum can be described by a single power law function. The best-fitting model for the non-simultaneous spectrum between 1.4 and 237 GHz is $S_{\nu} = (0.32\pm0.08) \nu^{-0.12\pm0.08}$." (Nature of the components E and C)
   - validation: Compared derived spectral index of E (-0.64 ± 0.05) with earlier arcsec-resolution measurement (-0.60 ± 0.08) by Ho & Ulvestad (2001).  
  > "Assuming no significant time variability, we can derive a spectral index of $\alpha=-0.64\pm0.05$ for the component E in Fig. 4. This is in agreement with the early report $\alpha=-0.60\pm0.08$ observed by (cite: Ho2001) between 1.4 and 5 GHz with their arcsec-resolution images." (Multi-frequency imaging results)

## Qualitative findings

- Feature E is offset by ~218 mas (4.6 pc) from the Gaia optical position of the accreting IMBH, has a steep spectrum, shows diffuse structure at 1.4 GHz, and is undetected at 5 GHz with the EVN, indicating it traces nuclear shocks rather than a continuous jet base.  
  > "Together with the optically thin steep spectrum and the extremely large angular offset (about 220 mas) from the accurate optical *Gaia* position, we explain the feature E as nuclear shocks likely formed by the IMBH’s episodic ejection or wide-angle outflow." (Abstract)
- A sub-mJy pc-scale diffuse component C is detected at the Gaia IMBH position in both VLA (12–18 GHz) and ALMA (237 GHz) images, with a flat broad-band spectrum and low brightness temperature, consistent with thermal free-free emission from a clumpy torus.  
  > "The VLA and ALMA observations find a sub-mJy pc-scale diffuse feature, possibly tracing a thermal free-free emission region near the IMBH." (Abstract); "Because of its relatively flat spectrum and VLBI non-detections at 1.4 and 5 GHz, we tentatively interpreted the central pc-scale feature C as a thermal emission region originating from a clumpy torus." (Conclusions)
- No compact radio core (jet base) is detected at the IMBH position in 1.4 GHz HSA or 5 GHz EVN images, implying no evidence of sub-pc-scale disc-jet coupling and placing the VLBI-scale core >=0.8 dex below the Merloni et al. (2003) fundamental plane relation.  
  > "There is no detection of a jet base at the IMBH position in the VLBI maps. The non-detections give an extremely low luminosity of $\leq4.7\times10^{33}$ erg s$^{-1}$ at 5 GHz and indicate no evidence of a disc-jet coupling on sub-pc scales." (Abstract); "Because of the relatively large radio luminosity offset ($\ge$0.8 dex) from the fundamental plane relation of (cite: Merloni2003) at 5 GHz, the data point of NGC 4395 in the mass gap tends to support the recent VLBI results reported by (cite: Fischer2021)." (Implications)

## Reasoning

- Why this data: To investigate whether the elongated 1.4 GHz feature E includes a flat-spectrum jet base powered by the central accreting IMBH, high-resolution 5 GHz EVN VLBI data were combined with 2008 1.4 GHz HSA data, 12–18 GHz VLA data, and 237.1 GHz ALMA data.  
  > "To investigate whether the elongated feature includes a jet base powered by the central accreting IMBH, we conducted VLBI observations with the EVN at 5.0 GHz and re-analysed the existing archival data observed with the HSA at 1.4 GHz in 2008, the Jansky VLA at 12–18 GHz in 2016 and the Atacama Large Millimetre/submillimetre Array (ALMA) at 237.1 GHz in 2018–2019." (Introduction)
- Why this method: Phase-referencing VLBI with ICRF3/Gaia calibrators and a secondary nearby VLASS calibrator was used to achieve ~1 mas astrometric precision relative to the Gaia optical frame, while Gaussian/point-source visibility model fitting was used to recover diffuse structure and measure component sizes and fluxes.  
  > "To improve the phase-referencing calibration further, we also observed a faint source VLASS J122755.50$+$334526.9 (cite: Gordon2021) as a nearby calibrator for one 2.5-min scan per half hour." (The EVN experiment at 5 GHz); "Compared to the CLEAN image, this image is made with the model fitting and recovers slightly more diffuse emission on the short baselines and plainly displays the large-scale diffuse emission." (Multi-frequency imaging results)
- Alternative: Ionisation of the nuclear region by star formation or a compact starburst as the origin of component E: rejected because [OIII]/Hbeta is 5.5–11.0 peaking at the optical centroid, the star-formation rate is very low (0.03 M_sun yr^-1), T_b ~ 10^6 K is too high, and there is no evidence of nuclear starbursts.  
  > "This supports the entire nuclear region hosting the components C and E being ionised by the central accreting IMBH instead of by star formation (cite: Brum2019)." (Nature of the components E and C); "The possibility of it being a compact starburst has been excluded because of its high brightness temperature $\sim$10$^6$ K and no evidence of starbursts in the nuclear region (cite: Brum2019)." (Nature of the components E and C)
- Alternative: Young supernova or supernova remnant as the origin of component E: rejected due to the relatively stable flux density over about four decades and its non-circular structure.  
  > "Due to the relatively stable flux density and the non-circular structure, the component E cannot be naturally identified as a young supernova or a supernova remnant." (Nature of the components E and C)
- Alternative: Partially optically thick synchrotron jet base as the origin of the flat-spectrum component C: rejected because a synchrotron jet base would have a high brightness temperature (>10^6 K) and conical structure, whereas C is extended (143 mas), has T_b = 56 ± 9 K at 15 GHz, and is undetected on VLBI scales.  
  > "A flat radio spectrum can also come from a partially optically thick synchrotron jet base (cite: Blandford2019). However, the jet base will have a quite high brightness temperature of $>10^{6}$ K and show a conical structure." (Nature of the components E and C)
