## Analysis recipe

1. **Calibrate visibilities, separate continuum, and image spectral cubes at common resolution**: Calibrate six archival datasets using standard JAO calibration scripts in CASA, separate continuum and line emission prior to CLEAN, CLEAN all cubes with a common 1.36 arcsec circular restoring beam, and smooth the J = 99-98 cubes from 244 kHz to 488 kHz spectral resolution.  
  > "Before the stacking, the data sets were calibrated by using the standard calibration scripts provided by the Joint ALMA Observaroty (JAO) ." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking); "Since each observation has a different spectral/spatial resolution, they were newly CLEANed with a common synthesized beam size—a circular beam with a 1 . 36 in diameter—instead of using the QA2 CLEANed images originally stored in the ALMA data archive." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking); "Furthermore, because the J = 99–98 data has a fine spectral resolution of 244 kHz, the resolution was smoothed to 488 kHz to match that of the lower one." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking)
   - software: CASA
   - parameter: Synthesized beam = 1.36 arcsec circular  
  > "Since each observation has a different spectral/spatial resolution, they were newly CLEANed with a common synthesized beam size—a circular beam with a 1 . 36 in diameter—instead of using the QA2 CLEANed images originally stored in the ALMA data archive." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking)
   - parameter: Spectral resolution = 488 kHz  
  > "Furthermore, because the J = 99–98 data has a fine spectral resolution of 244 kHz, the resolution was smoothed to 488 kHz to match that of the lower one." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking)
   - fitting: _not stated_
   - validation: _not stated_
2. **Extract disk-averaged spectra and stack transitions**: Extract disk-averaged spectra from the disk center position determined from the continuum emission map, integrate the six spectra by transition (two for J=96-95, four for J=99-98), and combine the two transitions using noise-weighted averaging based on rms measured in line-absent spectral regions.  
  > "Disk-averaged spectra were extracted from the disk center position determined by the continuum emission map. Six independent spectra were integrated according to the transition." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking); "Integration of two HC 5 N transitions was performed using the weighted averaging method according to the noise level measured in the line-absent spectral area of the data sets as 21 and 23 mK for J = 96–95 and J = 99–98 data, respectively." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking)
   - parameter: Titan apparent disk size = 0.65 arcsec  
  > "The adopted beam size is about twice the Titan apparent disk size (0 . 65), thus the spectra analyzed in this study correspond to disk-averaged ones." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking)
   - parameter: Noise weights from line-free rms: 21 mK (J=96-95) and 23 mK (J=99-98)  
  > "Integration of two HC 5 N transitions was performed using the weighted averaging method according to the noise level measured in the line-absent spectral area of the data sets as 21 and 23 mK for J = 96–95 and J = 99–98 data, respectively." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking)
   - assumption: No temporal variation of HC5N abundance during the 14-day observation period  
  > "While it is well known that the abundances of trace species on Titan exhibit large temporal variations, we assume no such variation for HC 5 N during the 14 days observation period." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking)
   - fitting: _not stated_
   - validation: Measured rms noise in line-free region of stacked spectrum is 15 mK (~0.9 mJy beam^-1)  
  > "The achieved noise level was measured to be as low as 15 mK in the brightness temperature scale, ∼0.9 mJy beam –1 in the flux density scale." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking)
3. **Radiative transfer modeling and derivation of HC5N upper limits and [HC5N]/[HC3N] ratios**: Calculate synthetic spectra for J = 96-95 and J = 99-98 separately using the Planetary Spectrum Generator (PSG) for four literature a priori vertical abundance profiles and a two-layer model, co-add with the same weighting as observations, and scale each profile by a multiplication factor so that the synthesized peak emission equals 3 times the observed line-free rms noise.  
  > "To investigate the upper limit abundance of HC 5 N, we conducted a radiative transfer analysis using the Planetary Spectrum Generator (PSG; G. L. Villanueva et al. 2018)." (2.2. Radiative Transfer Modeling of HC 5 N); "The synthesized spectra corresponding to the multiple HC 5 N transitions of J = 96–95 and 99–98 were calculated separately by the PSG and then added together with the same weighting used in the integration of observed spectra." (2.2. Radiative Transfer Modeling of HC 5 N); "Since the HC 5 N exhibits emission structure, we measured the synthesized peak emission intensity and derived the corresponding multiplication factor required to achieve three times the intensity of the root mean square noise level measured in a line-free frequency region as mentioned in the previous section." (2.2. Radiative Transfer Modeling of HC 5 N)
   - software: Planetary Spectrum Generator (PSG)
   - parameter: 600 altitude layers from 0 to 1200 km with 2 km step  
  > "The structure was layered into 600 altitude levels ranging from 0 to 1200 km with a step of 2 km." (2.2. Radiative Transfer Modeling of HC 5 N)
   - assumption: Pressure broadening coefficient = 0.07 cm^-1 atm^-1 with power-law temperature dependence of 0.05  
  > "coefficient, 0.07 cm [−][1] atm [−][1] with a power-law temperature dependence of 0.05 was assumed." (2.2 Radiative Transfer Modeling of HC 5 N)
   - assumption: Atmospheric temperature profile from Thelen et al. (2018), extrapolated above ~10^-6 bar  
  > "For the atmospheric model, we used an atmospheric temperature profile inferred from recent ALMA observations by A. E. Thelen et al. (2018). For the upper atmospheric structure, which is not explicitly shown in A. E. Thelen et al. (2018) paper, we extrapolated the temperature at ∼10 −6 bar as shown in Figure 3." (2.2. Radiative Transfer Modeling of HC 5 N)
   - assumption: Collision Induced Absorption of N2-N2, N2-CH4, and CH4-CH4 from HITRAN 2016  
  > "To reproduce the baseline emission, the Collision Induced Absorption spectra of N 2 –N 2, N 2 –CH 4 and CH 4 –CH 4 provided by the HITRAN 2016 database (H. M. Picket et al. 1998) was applied." (2.2. Radiative Transfer Modeling of HC 5 N)
   - fitting: Analytical scaling (multiplication factor) of a priori profiles to match 3 sigma rms peak intensity  
  > "The upper limit abundance was derived as a multiplication factor of four a priori models." (2.2. Radiative Transfer Modeling of HC 5 N)
   - validation: _not stated_

## Qualitative findings

- No statistically significant detection of HC5N spectral features in Titan's atmosphere after integrating ~15,000 s of ALMA Band 6 data.  
  > "Integrating six independent observation data sets with a total integration time of ∼15,000 s, we found no statistically significant detection of an HC 5 N spectral feature." (abstract)
- The photochemical models of Lellouch et al. (2010b) and Krasnopolsky (2009) significantly overestimate the atmospheric HC5N column density, whereas the models of Loison et al. (2015) and Vuitton et al. (2019) are consistent with the nondetection.  
  > "The nondetection of HC 5 N is found to be consistent with the photochemical model profile from J. C. Loison et al. (2015) and V. Vuitton et al. (2019), but not with the model of both E. Lellouch et al. (2010b) and V. A. Krasnopolsky (2009)—the latter models significantly overestimates the atmospheric HC 5 N column density." (4. Conclusion)
- The upper limit [HC5N]/[HC3N] ratio on Titan derived for three of four models is lower than that measured in high-mass protostellar objects, implying less efficient synthesis of longer carbon chains on Titan, possibly due to lower C4H2 abundance.  
  > "The corresponding upper limit of [HC 5 N]/[HC 3 N] ratio value derived from three of four models seem to be lower than that measured in high-mass protostellar objects, possibly due to the lower abundance of C 4 H 2 ." (abstract); "The relatively low [HC 5 N]/[HC 3 N] ratio we found implies that synthesis of progressively longer carbon chains is less efficient on Titan than in the cold interstellar medium and protostellar envelopes." (4. Conclusion)

## Reasoning

- Why this data: Project 2019.1.00783.S covered several HC5N transitions and had long integration times (2000–3000 s per execution), providing the highest achievable sensitivity ever for Titan in this frequency range.  
  > "We found out the data sets also include several HC 5 N transitions. In addition, since the integration time of each measurement was long (2000–3000 s), achievable total sensitivity was the highest ever for Titan, in this frequency range." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking)
- Why this method: Spectral stacking integrates multiple observations and transitions to improve sensitivity to weak emission lines; re-CLEANing to a common 1.36 arcsec circular beam and smoothing to 488 kHz accounts for differing native spatial and spectral resolutions across the six datasets.  
  > "To identify the weak HC 5 N emission line, the spectral stacking technique (e.g., L. Neumann et al. 2023), which integrates multiple spectra acquired in different measurements, was employed." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking); "Since each observation has a different spectral/spatial resolution, they were newly CLEANed with a common synthesized beam size—a circular beam with a 1 . 36 in diameter—instead of using the QA2 CLEANed images originally stored in the ALMA data archive." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking)
- Alternative: Using QA2 CLEANed archive images directly was rejected in favor of re-CLEANing to a common 1.36 arcsec circular beam because each observation had a different native spatial and spectral resolution.  
  > "Since each observation has a different spectral/spatial resolution, they were newly CLEANed with a common synthesized beam size—a circular beam with a 1 . 36 in diameter—instead of using the QA2 CLEANed images originally stored in the ALMA data archive." (2.1. Imaging and Spectral Stacking)
- Alternative: Two-layer vertical profile model was tested for comparison with Marten et al. (2002), but noted as physically unlikely compared to slope-like photochemical model profiles.  
  > "For comparison with their result, we have used a similar kind of the vertical profile (twolayer model) and obtained the upper limit as 13% of A. Marten et al. (2002). As stated in their paper, note that such a twolayer model is unlikely, and the slope-like vertical distribution is a more appropriate description." (3. Discussion)
- How they decided it was enough: _not stated_
- Limitation (authors): Current sensitivity cannot constrain the lower HC5N abundances predicted by the models of Loison et al. (2015) and Vuitton et al. (2019); detecting the emission predicted by Loison et al. (2015) does not appear feasible even with state-of-the-art observational techniques.  
  > "The result also indicates that deeper future observations will be required to effectively constrain other two models of J. C. Loison et al. (2015) and V. Vuitton et al. (2019), and to assess the validity of the lower predicted value." (3. Discussion); "Even with state-of-the-art observational techniques, detecting the weak HC 5 N emission predicted using the profile from J. C. Loison et al. (2015) does not appear feasible." (3. Discussion)
- Limitation (authors): Derived column density upper limits for the Vuitton et al. (2019) and Krasnopolsky (2009) models depend strongly on the assumed HC5N vertical profile in the lower atmosphere, which does not affect the spectral emission feature.  
  > "Because of the high abundance in the lower atmospheric region, which does not affect the emission feature, the model of V. Vuitton et al. (2019) has 11–51 times higher column density than other models." (2.2. Radiative Transfer Modeling of HC 5 N); "Note that the column density value of V. Vuitton et al. (2019) and V. A. Krasnopolsky (2009) highly depends on the HC 5 N vertical profile in the lower region." (3. Discussion)
- Caveat (inferred, ours): The HC3N column density used to compute [HC5N]/[HC3N] was taken from 2012 ALMA observations (Cordiner et al. 2014), whereas the HC5N data are from December 2019; seasonal variations in Titan's nitrile abundances between 2012 and 2019 could affect the disk-averaged [HC5N]/[HC3N] ratio.
- Caveat (inferred, ours): The J = 96-95 and J = 99-98 transitions have high lower-state energies (E_L ~ 583 K and 620 K), making Band 6 observations relatively insensitive to cool stratospheric gas compared to lower-J transitions at lower frequencies.
