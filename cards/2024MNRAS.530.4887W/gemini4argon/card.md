## Analysis recipe

1. **Compile sample and filter by photometric coverage**: Select HerBS and SPT DSFGs with spectroscopic redshifts and restrict to sources having at least two photometric measurements at observed wavelengths beyond 1 mm; combine ALMA multiple components within Herschel beams if \Delta z < 0.1.  
  > "For this study, we have retained the HerBS sources which are multiples in the ALMA images, if the redshifts of the sources are within 0.1 of each other (we have taken the average redshift as that of the system)." (2.1 HerBS sub-sample); "However, as we are interested in estimating the galaxy-integrated \beta of each source, which is characterised by the slope of the dust emission on the R-J side of the Planck function, we restrict our final sample to those galaxies that have at least two photometric measurements at observed wavelengths beyond 1 mm." (2.3 Restrictions on sample for this study); "This reduces our sample to a total of 109 galaxies (79 SPT DSFGs, plus 30 from HerBS/BEARS)." (2.3 Restrictions on sample for this study)
   - parameter: Minimum 2 photometric points at \lambda_{obs} > 1 mm; \Delta z < 0.1 for multiples  
  > "For this study, we have retained the HerBS sources which are multiples in the ALMA images, if the redshifts of the sources are within 0.1 of each other (we have taken the average redshift as that of the system)." (2.1 HerBS sub-sample); "However, as we are interested in estimating the galaxy-integrated \beta of each source, which is characterised by the slope of the dust emission on the R-J side of the Planck function, we restrict our final sample to those galaxies that have at least two photometric measurements at observed wavelengths beyond 1 mm." (2.3 Restrictions on sample for this study)
   - assumption: Multiple ALMA sources within the Herschel beam with only one spectroscopic redshift are physically connected at that redshift.  
  > "If there are multiple sources in the ALMA image within the Herschel beam but a spectroscopic redshift for only one source, we have assumed the sources are physical connected and used the redshift of the one source as the redshift of the system." (2.1 HerBS sub-sample)
   - fitting: _not stated_
   - validation: _not stated_
2. **Fit FIR-to-mm SEDs with CMB-adjusted modified blackbody models**: Fit a single modified blackbody combined with a mid-IR power-law (corrected for CMB heating and background contrast) using MCMC under four opacity cases: optically thin, \lambda_1 = 100 \mu m, \lambda_1 = 200 \mu m, and fixed physical area (for 37 SPT sources).  
  > "We model the FIR-to-millimetre spectra of our galaxies with a single modified blackbody (MBB) model combined with a mid-IR power-law, where the MBB represents the emission from the reservoir of cold dust, which represents most of the dust in the ISM, and the power-law represents emission from any dust in the vicinity of star-forming regions, young OB stars, or active galactic nuclei (AGN)." (3 SED FITTING); "We therefore also used two models in which the dust becomes optically thick at wavelengths below \lambda_1 = 100 \mu m or 200 \mu m." (3 SED FITTING); "For a sub-sample of 37 galaxies, we have measurements of the sizes of the sources, which allows us to estimate the opacity directly." (6 CONCLUSIONS)
   - parameter: Free parameters: M_{dust}, T_{dust}, \beta, \alpha (and \nu_1 in general opacity models); L_{IR} integrated over rest-frame 8-1000 \mu m  
  > "Using this general form of the MBB, there are up to four free parameters: the dust mass, the dust temperature, T_{dust}, the dust emissivity index, \beta, and the frequency at which the dust opacity reaches unity." (3 SED FITTING); "Stacked posterior distributions of log(\mu M_{dust}), T_{dust}, \beta, log(\mu L_{IR}) (rest-frame 8–1000 \mu m) and \lambda_{peak} for SPT (top panels) and HerBS galaxies (bottom panels)." (Figure 2 caption)
   - assumption: \Lambda CDM cosmology with H_0 = 67.7 km s^-1 Mpc^-1 and \Omega_m = 0.31  
  > "Throughout this paper we assume a \Lambda CDM cosmology with H_0 = 67.7 km s^{-1} Mpc^{-1} and \Omega_m = 0.31 (Planck Collaboration et al. 2020)." (1 INTRODUCTION)
   - assumption: \kappa_0 = 0.077 m^2 kg^-1 at \nu_0 = 353 GHz; T_{CMB,0} = 2.73 K; \mu = 1 when unlensed  
  > "In the following, we assume \kappa_0 = 0.077 m^2 kg^{-1} at \nu_0 = 353 GHz (Dunne et al. 2000; James et al. 2002)." (3 SED FITTING); "For sources where there is no evidence of gravitational lensing, we assume \mu = 1." (3 SED FITTING); "where T_{dust,0} is the dust temperature as observed at z = 0 and T_{CMB,0} = 2.73 K is the CMB temperature at z = 0." (3 SED FITTING)
   - assumption: Calibration errors added in quadrature: 7% (PACS), 5.5% (SPIRE), 5% (SCUBA-2), 12% (LABOCA), 7% (SPT), 10% (ALMA)  
  > "assuming absolute calibrations of 7 per cent ( _Herschel_ -PACS), 5.5 per cent ( _Herschel_ -SPIRE), 5 per cent (SCUBA-2), 12 per cent (APEX-LABOCA), 7 per cent (SPT) and 10 per cent (ALMA)." (3 SED fitting)
   - fitting: MCMC with flat priors on all free parameters; best-fitting value taken as the median (50th percentile) and 1\sigma uncertainties as the 16th and 84th percentiles of the posterior distribution.  
  > "During SED fitting we assume flat priors on all free parameters and take the best-fitting SED to be the median value of the posterior distribution for each parameter." (3 SED FITTING); "The (1-\sigma) uncertainties are quoted at the 16th and 84th percentiles." (3 SED FITTING)
   - validation: Comparison of optically thin and general opacity models against the 37 SPT sources with directly measured continuum sizes, plus reduced chi-squared (\chi^2_\nu) reported in Tables B1 and B2.  
  > "The probability distributions for the optically-thin model are a good match to those for the subset of galaxies for which we know the size of the dust region, and therefore the parameter estimates we discuss in the rest of this paper have been derived using the optically-thin model." (4.1 Comparison between the optically thin and general opacity models)
3. **Quantify correlation between beta and dust temperature**: Calculate Pearson (r_{Pearson}) and Spearman rank (\rho_{Spearman}) correlation coefficients between \beta and T_{dust} for the optically-thin model fits.  
  > "The strength of these correlations were tested with the Pearson correlation coefficient, r_{Pearson}, and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, \rho_{Spearman}." (4.2 The \beta–dust temperature relationship)
   - assumption: Optically thin dust emission  
  > "Fig. 4 shows that there is a negative correlation between the measured values of \beta and dust temperature for both sub-samples when assuming that the dust is optically thin." (4.2 The \beta–dust temperature relationship)
   - fitting: _not stated_
   - validation: Comparison with simulated mock SEDs to separate correlated measurement errors from intrinsic correlation.  
  > "The large differences between the r.m.s. values for the simulation and for the real samples imply there is a genuine inverse correlation between \beta and T_{dust} which is not caused by correlated measurement errors." (4.3 Simulations)
4. **Simulate mock SEDs to assess parameter recovery, correlated errors, and intrinsic scatter in beta**: Generate mock SEDs from the optically-thin model with parameters drawn from uniform distributions, add normal observational errors, apply survey flux detection limits (>80 mJy at 500 \mu m for HerBS; >25 mJy at 870 \mu m for SPT), and refit with the same SED fitting routine.  
  > "A catalogue of models were produced with dust parameters randomly drawn from a uniform distribution between the lower and upper bounds in Table 2" (4.3 Simulations); "We created observed SEDs of each mock galaxy by adding flux errors to the model SED, drawing randomly from a normal distribution with a standard deviation equal to the real observational error at that wavelength." (4.3 Simulations); "Once we had calculated an observed SED for each mock galaxy, we removed it from the sample if it fell below the detection limits of the real samples (Section 2)." (4.3 Simulations)
   - parameter: Flat bounds: log(\mu L_{IR}) = 13-14 (HerBS) and 13-14.5 (SPT), T_{dust} = 20-50 K, \beta = 0.5-4 (or 1\sigma range 1.68-2.41 for SPT and 1.61-2.26 for HerBS in Section 5.2), \alpha = 1-5; z = 2-4 (HerBS) and 2-6 (SPT)  
  > "log(\mu L_{IR} [L_\odot]) 13–14 (HerBS) 13–14.5 (SPT) T_{dust} [K] 20–50 \beta 0.5–4 \alpha 1–5" (Table 2); "To create the first sample, we placed each galaxy at a random redshift between 2 and 4 and gave the galaxy the same photometric coverage as for the HerBS sub-sample, omitting photometry at 1.2 mm as only a few of the HerBS sources have photometry at this wavelength." (4.3 Simulations); "This time we used values of \beta drawn from a uniform distribution covering the \pm 1\sigma range of the observed values in Fig. 6, generating – as before – two samples of mock galaxies with observational data similar to that of the SPT and HerBS sub-samples." (5.2 Variation in \beta)
   - assumption: Lensing magnification \mu = 5.5 for mock SPT galaxies and \mu = 5.3 for mock HerBS galaxies  
  > "We assumed a lensing magnification of 5.5\times for all SPT galaxies and 5.3\times for all HerBS galaxies, equal to the median lensing magnification for the two sub-samples." (4.3 Simulations)
   - fitting: Same MCMC SED fitting procedure as applied to the real sample.  
  > "For the sources that remained, we then derived the dust parameters using the same modelling procedure we had applied to the real sample." (4.3 Simulations)
   - validation: Root-mean-square error (RMSE) between input and recovered parameters.  
  > "The r.m.s. error (RMSE) is calculated for each dust parameter, showing the intrinsic scatter around the ‘true’ input values." (4.3 Simulations)
5. **Test whether interferometric flux loss at 3 mm explains extreme beta values**: Repeat SED fitting without the 3-mm ALMA photometry and with fixed \beta = 2 for five extreme-\beta SPT sources, comparing predicted and observed 3-mm flux densities.  
  > "We assessed how much would need to be lost by repeating the SED fitting without the 3-mm photometry, with \beta = 2." (5.2 Variation in \beta); "We did this for five sources: SPT0112-55 (\beta = 3.62), SPT0611-55 (\beta = 3.44), SPT2203-41 (\beta = 2.84), SPT2349-52 (\beta = 3.62) and SPT2357-51 (\beta = 2.80)." (5.2 Variation in \beta)
   - parameter: \beta = 2 fixed; 3-mm photometry omitted  
  > "We assessed how much would need to be lost by repeating the SED fitting without the 3-mm photometry, with \beta = 2." (5.2 Variation in \beta)
   - fitting: SED fitting with fixed \beta = 2  
  > "We assessed how much would need to be lost by repeating the SED fitting without the 3-mm photometry, with \beta = 2." (5.2 Variation in \beta)
   - validation: Compare predicted 3-mm flux density to observed 3-mm flux density.  
  > "Comparing the flux density at 3 mm predicted by the model with the observed flux density, we find that the observed flux density would need to be between 60–80 per cent lower than the true flux density to explain the very high values of \beta." (5.2 Variation in \beta)
6. **Measure evolution of dust temperature with redshift at fixed IR luminosity**: Select a luminosity-limited subset of galaxies, calculate peak dust temperature T_{peak} from \lambda_{peak} using Wien's displacement law, and perform bootstrap fitting of a linear model of T_{peak} versus redshift.  
  > "To avoid this dependence, we have derived \lambda_{peak} for each source and then calculated T_{dust} from Wien’s displacement law." (5.4 Evolution of T_{dust} with redshift); "The bottom panel of Fig. 8 shows the peak dust temperature (T_{peak} = 2.898 \times 10^3 [\mu mK]/\lambda_{peak} [\mu m]) versus redshift." (5.4 Evolution of T_{dust} with redshift); "Bootstrap fitting with a linear model gives an evolution rate of 5.20 \pm 0.92 and -0.25 \pm 0.52 T_{peak}/z for the HerBS and SPT galaxies, respectively." (5.4 Evolution of T_{dust} with redshift)
   - parameter: T_{peak} = 2.898 x 10^3 [\mu m K] / \lambda_{peak} [\mu m]; detection limits calculated with T_{dust} = 32 K and \beta = 2  
  > "In calculating the detection limits, we have assumed an SED with a dust temperature of 32 K, the median temperature of the sample, and \beta = 2." (5.4 Evolution of T_{dust} with redshift); "The bottom panel of Fig. 8 shows the peak dust temperature (T_{peak} = 2.898 \times 10^3 [\mu mK]/\lambda_{peak} [\mu m]) versus redshift." (5.4 Evolution of T_{dust} with redshift)
   - assumption: Wien's displacement law relates \lambda_{peak} to T_{peak} independent of dust opacity model.  
  > "The dust temperature derived from SED fitting depends on the model assumed for the dust opacity, whereas \lambda_{peak} is the same for every model. To avoid this dependence, we have derived \lambda_{peak} for each source and then calculated T_{dust} from Wien’s displacement law." (5.4 Evolution of T_{dust} with redshift)
   - fitting: Bootstrap fitting with a linear model  
  > "Bootstrap fitting with a linear model gives an evolution rate of 5.20 \pm 0.92 and -0.25 \pm 0.52 T_{peak}/z for the HerBS and SPT galaxies, respectively." (5.4 Evolution of T_{dust} with redshift)

## Qualitative findings

- Estimates of the dust emissivity index (\beta), SED peak wavelength (\lambda_{peak}), and infrared luminosity (L_{IR}) are insensitive to opacity assumptions, whereas dust temperature (T_{dust}) and dust mass (M_{dust}) depend strongly on the assumed opacity model.  
  > "We find that our estimates of \beta, the peak of the galaxy SED, and the far-infrared luminosity, are similar between the models, although there is a change in \beta of 0.2 between the most extreme models." (6 CONCLUSIONS); "In contrast, our estimates of dust mass and dust temperature depend strongly on the opacity assumptions." (6 CONCLUSIONS)
- There is no evidence for evolution in \beta across 2 < z < 6, and the observed scatter in \beta reflects genuine intrinsic variations in dust properties among DSFGs rather than measurement error.  
  > "We find no evidence of a change in \beta with redshift, with a median value of \beta = 1.96 for the optically-thin model with a confidence interval (16–84%) of 1.67 to 2.35 for the population." (abstract); "Using simulations, we estimate the measurement errors from our procedure and show that the variation of \beta in the population results from intrinsic variations in the properties of the dust in DSFGs." (abstract)
- There is a genuine inverse correlation between \beta and T_{dust} in DSFGs that cannot be explained solely by correlated measurement errors.  
  > "After allowing for the effects of correlated measurement errors, we find an inverse correlation between \beta and T_{dust} in DSFGs, for which there is also evidence in low-redshift galaxies." (abstract); "The large differences between the r.m.s. values for the simulation and for the real samples imply there is a genuine inverse correlation between \beta and T_{dust} which is not caused by correlated measurement errors." (4.3 Simulations)
- At a fixed far-infrared luminosity, there is no strong evidence for evolution in dust temperature with redshift across the combined sample.  
  > "At a fixed far-infrared luminosity, we find no evidence for a change in dust temperature, T_{dust}, with redshift." (abstract); "Given the lack of any obvious correlation when both sub-samples are considered together, we conclude that there is no strong evidence for temperature evolution." (5.4 Evolution of T_{dust} with redshift)

## Reasoning

- Why this data: Measuring galaxy-integrated \beta requires constraining the Rayleigh-Jeans slope of the dust emission alongside the SED peak, so the sample was restricted to galaxies with spectroscopic redshifts and at least two photometric points at observed wavelengths > 1 mm (provided by ALMA and SPT).  
  > "The SPT sources have photometry between 250 \mu m and 3 mm, corresponding to a rest-frame range of 86 \mu m \lesssim \lambda_{rest} \lesssim 380 \mu m, meaning that the peak of the dust emission (\sim 100 \mu m) and Rayleigh-Jeans (R-J) tail are well sampled." (2.2 SPT sub-sample); "However, as we are interested in estimating the galaxy-integrated \beta of each source, which is characterised by the slope of the dust emission on the R-J side of the Planck function, we restrict our final sample to those galaxies that have at least two photometric measurements at observed wavelengths beyond 1 mm." (2.3 Restrictions on sample for this study)
- Why this method: Tested both optically-thin and general-opacity MBB models (including CMB corrections) because compact DSFGs may be optically thick at FIR wavelengths, used mock SED simulations to disentangle correlated fitting errors from intrinsic \beta-T_{dust} correlations, and used Wien's law T_{peak} in a luminosity-limited box to test temperature evolution without opacity model dependence or luminosity-temperature bias.  
  > "However, since in DSFGs a large amount of dust is packed into a small region (e.g. Ikarashi et al. 2017), it is possible that the dust is optically thick at one or more of the wavelengths covered by our photometry (e.g. Conley et al. 2011; Casey et al. 2019; Cortzen et al. 2020)." (3 SED FITTING); "The dust temperature derived from SED fitting depends on the model assumed for the dust opacity, whereas \lambda_{peak} is the same for every model. To avoid this dependence, we have derived \lambda_{peak} for each source and then calculated T_{dust} from Wien’s displacement law." (5.4 Evolution of T_{dust} with redshift); "To avoid the selection effect caused by the luminosity–temperature relationship, we only consider sources selected in a small range of IR luminosity, as illustrated by the boxed regions." (5.4 Evolution of T_{dust} with redshift)
- Alternative: General opacity modified blackbody models with fixed \lambda_1 = 100 \mu m and \lambda_1 = 200 \mu m were fitted and compared, with \lambda_1 = 200 \mu m rejected as a poor match to the subset of galaxies with known continuum sizes, and the optically-thin model adopted for the main analysis.  
  > "This distribution agrees well with the probability distributions for the optically-thin model and for the general-opacity model with \lambda_1 = 100 \mu m, but not for the one with \lambda_1 = 200 \mu m." (4.1 Comparison between the optically thin and general opacity models); "The probability distributions for the optically-thin model are a good match to those for the subset of galaxies for which we know the size of the dust region, and therefore the parameter estimates we discuss in the rest of this paper have been derived using the optically-thin model." (4.1 Comparison between the optically thin and general opacity models)
- Alternative: Interferometric flux loss at 3 mm ('zero baseline problem') was considered and rejected as the primary explanation for extreme \beta \simeq 3 values in SPT galaxies because 60-80% of the 3-mm flux would need to be missed.  
  > "we find that the observed flux density would need to be between 60–80 per cent lower than the true flux density to explain the very high values of" (5.2 Variation in β); "Comparing the flux density at 3 mm predicted by the model with the observed flux density, we find that the observed flux density would need to be between 60–80 per cent lower than the true flux density to explain the very high values of \beta." (5.2 Variation in \beta)
- How they decided it was enough: _not stated_
- Limitation (authors): The value of \lambda_1 derived from known source sizes is uncertain because it is inversely proportional to the assumed dust mass opacity \kappa_\nu, which is itself very uncertain.  
  > "However, we note that this value is inversely proportional to the value assumed for the dust opacity (\kappa_\nu) and since the opacity of interstellar dust is still very uncertain (Clark et al. 2016), the frequency at which the opacity is unity is also uncertain." (4.1 Comparison between the optically thin and general opacity models)
- Limitation (authors): For multiple ALMA sources within a single Herschel beam where only one source has a spectroscopic redshift, the sources are assumed to be physically connected at that redshift.  
  > "If there are multiple sources in the ALMA image within the Herschel beam but a spectroscopic redshift for only one source, we have assumed the sources are physical connected and used the redshift of the one source as the redshift of the system." (2.1 HerBS sub-sample)
- Limitation (authors): The accuracy of recovered T_{dust} decreases at higher temperatures because the SED peak shifts to shorter wavelengths that are less constrained by Herschel photometry.  
  > "The accuracy of the output T_{dust} decreases for higher temperatures as a result of the SED shifting to shorter wavelengths, meaning the peak is then less constrained by the Herschel photometry than at lower temperatures." (4.3 Simulations)
- Limitation (authors): At z ~ 4, the 500 \mu m selection wavelength of the HerBS sample lies close to the rest-frame peak of the MBB, introducing a selection bias towards higher dust temperatures and consequently lower \beta values.  
  > "At this redshift, the selection wavelength of HerBS, 500 \mu m, is close to the peak of the MBB, thus biasing us towards higher temperatures." (5.1 Evolution of \beta with redshift); "For the highest redshift sources in the HerBS sub-sample, the selection wavelength in the rest frame is close to the peak of the MBB, which leads to a selection bias towards sources with higher temperatures." (5.4 Evolution of T_{dust} with redshift)
- Caveat (inferred, ours): Single-temperature modified blackbody models may not capture multi-temperature dust components along the line of sight, where mixing of cold and warm dust could flatten the apparent Rayleigh-Jeans slope or bias beta-T_dust correlations.
- Caveat (inferred, ours): Differential magnification in gravitationally lensed sources (which dominate both SPT and HerBS samples) can distort the integrated SED if compact warm regions are magnified differently than extended cold dust reservoirs.
- Caveat (inferred, ours): Blending of multiple ALMA sources within the coarse Herschel SPIRE beams assumes identical SEDs or physical association across components.
