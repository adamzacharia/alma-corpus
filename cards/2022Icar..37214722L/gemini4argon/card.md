## Science

- Fields: solar_system, astrochemistry
- Question: Obtain spatially-resolved measurements of CO(3-2) and HCN(4-3) in Pluto's atmosphere to investigate vertical and horizontal variations in temperature and composition, and search for new atmospheric compounds, primarily hydrogen isocyanide (HNC).  
  > "We report on new observations of Pluto’s atmosphere with ALMA, performed in April and July/August 2017 with two main goals: (i) obtaining spatially-resolved measurements ($\sim$0.06” on the $\sim$0.15” disk subtended by Pluto and its atmosphere) of CO(3-2) and HCN(4-3) (ii) targetting new chemical compounds, primarily hydrogen isocyanide (HNC) ." (Abstract); "A specific advantage is that currently Pluto’s North pole is well positioned for viewing from Earth (sub-Earth latitude on Pluto $\sim$ 50-57$^\circ$ N over 2015-2020) enabling the investigation of equatorial-to-pole variations in the temperature and composition fields." (Introduction)

## Analysis recipe

1. **Calibrate visibilities, perform phase self-calibration, and fix the absolute flux scale**: Calibrate visibilities and apply velocity corrections using the CASA pipeline. Image continuum spectral windows, perform phase self-calibration in AIPS, apply solutions to line spectral windows, fit continuum visibilities, and rescale flux density scale so Pluto's brightness temperature is 33 K.  
  > "This included calibration and velocity corrections on the spectral visibilities, performed with the CASA pipeline (Muders et al., 2014)." (Observations and data reduction); "For all four observations, we then imaged the continuum spectral windows (combining the two, for the CO/HCN setup), and used that image to perform self-calibration to correct for atmospheric phase fluctuations, also applying the determined solutions to the spectral line spectral windows." (Observations and data reduction); "Because of this, we fixed the flux density scale such that the brightness temperature of Pluto was 33 K, as was found in our 2015 observations (Paper I; Butler et al. 2020)." (Observations and data reduction)
   - software: CASA, AIPS
   - parameter: Continuum flux density set to 17.5 mJy at CO(3-2), 18.3 mJy at HCN(4-3), and 11.1 mJy at HNC(3-2)  
  > "This brightness temperature results in a continuum flux density level of 17.5 mJy at CO(3-2), 18.3 mJy at HCN(4-3), and 11.1 mJy at HNC(3-2)." (Observations and data reduction)
   - assumption: Pluto continuum brightness temperature is constant at 33 K  
  > "Because of this, we fixed the flux density scale such that the brightness temperature of Pluto was 33 K, as was found in our 2015 observations (Paper I; Butler et al. 2020)." (Observations and data reduction)
   - fitting: Visibility fitting of continuum spectral windows in AIPS  
  > "We then fitted the self-calibrated visibilities to determine the continuum brightness temperature of Pluto and Charon" (Observations and data reduction)
   - validation: Estimated expected thermal emission decrease from heliocentric distance change over 2015-2017 is only 0.7%  
  > "While the continuum thermal emission of Pluto and Charon may vary with the system’s heliocentric distance $r_h$, a simple scaling of temperatures as $r^{-0.5}_h$ would lead to an expected temperature decrease of only 0.7%" (Observations and data reduction)
2. **Extract zero-spacing (total flux density) CO and HCN spectra via visibility fitting and generate image cubes**: Fit spectral line visibilities per channel over u-v range <= 1000 klambda (excluding Charon, forcing Pluto at phase center) to derive zero-spacing spectra for individual dates and combined data. Produce image cubes for combined CO/HCN data and HNC setup.  
  > "For the CO/HCN setup observations, we next fitted the visibilities in the spectral line windows on a per-channel basis, in order to determine the best value of the total flux density (a.k.a. zero spacing flux) as a function of channel." (Observations and data reduction); "For this fitting, we excluded the Charon contribution and fitted the visibilities over a maximum u-v range of 1000 k$\lambda$, forcing Pluto to be at phase center." (Observations and data reduction); "Finally we produced image cubes for all of the spectral windows, for the combined CO/HCN data, and the HNC observation." (Observations and data reduction)
   - software: AIPS, GILDAS
   - parameter: Maximum u-v range = 1000 klambda; Pluto continuum disk = 0.1009" with limb darkening exponent 0.1  
  > "fitted the visibilities over a maximum u-v range of 1000 k$\lambda$, forcing Pluto to be at phase center. Pluto’s continuum was described as emission from a 0.1009" disk with some limb darkening (0.1 exponent)." (Observations and data reduction)
   - parameter: Prescribed CO emission size = 1.01, 1.05, 1.40 x Pluto diameter at 5, 1, 0 MHz from line center; HCN emission size = 1.00, 1.40, 1.78 x Pluto diameter at 0.6, 0.5, 0.0 MHz  
  > "For the CO spectral window, the specified emission size was 1.01, 1.05, and 1.40 times the diameter of Pluto, respectively, at 5 MHz, 1 MHz, and 0 MHz from line center (those values were interpolated for intermediate frequency offsets). For HCN, the specified emission size was 1.00, 1.40, and 1.78 times the diameter of Pluto, respectively, at 0.6 MHz, 0.5 MHz, and 0.0 MHz from the line center." (Observations and data reduction)
   - parameter: Pixel size = 0.01" (CO/HCN cube), 0.1" (HNC cube); spectral binning factor = 4 (HNC setup)  
  > "The image cube for the CO/HCN data was made with $0.01\mbox{$^{\prime\prime}$}$ pixels, in order to subsample the synthesized beam sufficiently" (Observations and data reduction); "for the HNC setup, which did not spatially resolve Pluto (resolution $\sim$0.62$\mbox{$^{\prime\prime}$}$ x $\sim$0.45$\mbox{$^{\prime\prime}$}$), the image cube was made with $0.1\mbox{$^{\prime\prime}$}$ pixels, and the spectra were simply extracted at image center. The spectral channels were binned by a factor 4 to enhance S/N" (Observations and data reduction)
   - assumption: Channel-dependent emission sizes prescribed from image cube modeling  
  > "For the lines, we prescribed channel-dependent values of the emission size based on modeling of the image cube data (see below)." (Observations and data reduction)
   - fitting: Per-channel visibility fitting in AIPS and GILDAS  
  > "This was done in AIPS, but was also repeated in GILDAS, leaving the fitted diameter as a free parameter; this yielded consistent results." (Observations and data reduction)
   - validation: Repeated visibility fitting in GILDAS with diameter as a free parameter and compared spectra across the three individual observing dates  
  > "This was done in AIPS, but was also repeated in GILDAS, leaving the fitted diameter as a free parameter; this yielded consistent results." (Observations and data reduction); "Within noise level, the CO and HCN lines are the same for the three individual dates" (Observations and data reduction)
3. **Construct integrated emission maps and radial/quadrant emission profiles for continuum, CO, and HCN**: Integrate CO over +/-5 MHz and HCN over +/-1 MHz from line center, and take continuum over +/-10-40 MHz. Average spectra in oversampled radial bins of 0.007" globally and split into four azimuthal quadrants (low vs high latitudes, morning vs evening). Estimate uncertainties via Monte Carlo noise clones.  
  > "The CO and HCN emissions, expressed in mJy$\cdot$MHz/beam, are integrated over $\pm$5 MHz and $\pm$1 MHz, respectively, from line center, and the continuum (in mJy/beam) is taken in the immediate vicinity of the lines ($\pm$10-40 MHz from line center)." (CO and HCN: data presentation); "To visualize these effects in more details, we averaged spectra in (oversampled) bins of 0.007" in radial distance, and calculated the CO and HCN line areas for each bin." (CO and HCN: data presentation); "we split radially-averaged profiles twice in two groups, forming four “quadrants" (see Fig. 2)." (CO and HCN: data presentation)
   - parameter: Integration ranges: +/-5 MHz (CO), +/-1 MHz (HCN), +/-10-40 MHz (continuum); radial bin width = 0.007"  
  > "The CO and HCN emissions, expressed in mJy$\cdot$MHz/beam, are integrated over $\pm$5 MHz and $\pm$1 MHz, respectively, from line center, and the continuum (in mJy/beam) is taken in the immediate vicinity of the lines ($\pm$10-40 MHz from line center)." (CO and HCN: data presentation); "we averaged spectra in (oversampled) bins of 0.007" in radial distance" (CO and HCN: data presentation)
   - fitting: _not stated_
   - validation: Monte Carlo error estimation by adding random spectral noise to observed spectra to create clones  
  > "To estimate error bars on these areas, we generated, for each bin, a series of “clones" of the observed spectrum, adding random spectral noise at the relevant level, and calculating the line area for each of these clones; the rms dispersion in the resulting areas yielded the desired error bar." (CO and HCN: data presentation)
4. **Retrieve disk-averaged and spatially-resolved temperature profiles and CO mixing ratios**: Use a 200-layer (0-1450 km) spherical non-scattering radiative transfer code convolved with the synthesized beam, coupled with a constrained and regularized Conrath/Rodgers inversion algorithm over +/-30 MHz from CO line core. First invert 2017 zero-spacing and 2015 disk-center CO spectra, then invert 2017 spatially-resolved spectra on a 21x21 grid (0.1" x 0.1" with 0.01" sampling) under 4 CO/temperature scenarios, and construct zonal mean temperature fields.  
  > "The model, presented in Paper I (where all model details can be found), was extended to cover the 0-1450 km altitude range by means of 200 layers, with an altitude-varying layer thickness." (CO and HCN: modelling); "The inversion scheme was applied to the measured fluxes (in Jy or Jy/beam) over $\pm$30 MHz from the CO line core (i.e. 345.766–345.826 GHz)." (Temperature / CO retrievals); "To ensure comfortable beam oversampling, CO spectra were inverted over a grid of 0.1” x 0.1” with 0.01” sampling (i.e. 21 x 21 spectra to invert)." (Temperature / CO retrievals)
   - parameter: 200 atmospheric layers from 0 to 1450 km; fitting interval = +/-30 MHz (345.766-345.826 GHz); maximum 2 iterations (1 iteration if rms decreases by < 2%)  
  > "The model, presented in Paper I (where all model details can be found), was extended to cover the 0-1450 km altitude range by means of 200 layers, with an altitude-varying layer thickness." (CO and HCN: modelling); "The inversion scheme was applied to the measured fluxes (in Jy or Jy/beam) over $\pm$30 MHz from the CO line core (i.e. 345.766–345.826 GHz)." (Temperature / CO retrievals); "the inversion process was limited to a maximum of two iterations, and even only one if the rms between the model and the data, calculated over the fitting interval, had decreased by less than 2 % after the first iteration." (Temperature / CO retrievals)
   - parameter: A priori for disk-averaged retrieval: CO = 515 ppm, temperature = New Horizons profile (Young et al., 2018); A priori for spatially-resolved retrieval: CO = 600 ppm, temperature = 2017 disk-integrated retrieved profile  
  > "In doing so, we initialized the CO mixing ratio to 515 ppm (Paper I). For the a priori temperatures, we initially used the New Horizons derived thermal profile (cite: young18)" (Temperature / CO retrievals); "In all cases, a 600 ppm CO mixing ratio and the disk-integrated temperature profile retrieved previously were used as a priori." (Temperature / CO retrievals)
   - assumption: CO mixing ratio is vertically uniform; surface brightness temperature is 33.0 K  
  > "A CO mixing ratio of 515 ppm (Paper I) and a brightness surface temperature of 33.0 K were used." (Initial fitting attempts of the emission radial profiles); "The method involves the initialization of the CO mixing ratio (assumed constant with altitude) and the temperature profile to initial (“a priori”) values." (Temperature / CO retrievals)
   - fitting: Constrained and regularized iterative inversion (Conrath et al., 1998; Rodgers, 2000) evaluated with chi^2 over +/-30 MHz and reduced chi^2 (S) over +/-10 MHz  
  > "Following the approach of Paper I, we analyzed the CO data using a constrained and regularized inversion method based on algorithms detailed in (cite: conrath98) and (cite: rodgers00)." (Temperature / CO retrievals); "The fitting quality of the a priori and of the solution were quantified by $\chi^2$ statistics, both in unreduced form ($\chi^2$ = $\Sigma$ \[(model$_i$-obs$_i$)/$\sigma_i$\] $^2$ where $i$ refers to the $N$ independent frequencies in the $\pm$30 MHz fitting interval) and in reduced form $S$ = $\sqrt{\chi'^2/N'}$ (in this case, $\chi'^2$ was calculated over the $N'$ frequencies within $\pm$10 MHz" (Temperature / CO retrievals)
   - validation: Tested 4 inversion variants (fixed CO=600 ppm; fixed T; free CO and T; fixed spatially-variable CO=800-4*lambda ppm), two zonal-mean averaging methods, and an alternate 3-unit physical surface temperature model (Lellouch et al., 2016)  
  > "The variants, shown from left to right in Fig. 6, include the following cases: (1) fixed, uniform, CO = 600 ppm, free thermal profile (2) fixed thermal profile, free CO (3) free CO and thermal profiles (4) fixed, but spatially variable CO, free thermal profiles." (Temperature / CO retrievals); "We used an alternate surface temperature description, based on the physical (but still pre- New Horizons) model of (cite: lellouch16)." (Temperature / CO retrievals); "This process however led to atmospheric temperature fields that were not significantly different from those inferred with a constant surface temperature model, leading us to believe our result is also robust in this respect." (Temperature / CO retrievals)
5. **Determine the mean vertical profile of HCN and search for spatial variations in HCN**: Run a parametric forward-model grid varying lower-atmosphere scaling f_low, top altitude z_top, top mixing ratio q_top, and shape exponent alpha. Fit to 2017 radial HCN line areas (9 bins of 0.014"), 2017 zero-spacing line, and 2015 disk-center line. Then invert spatially-resolved HCN spectra at each beam position (0.01" grid) by fitting a constant scaling factor to the nominal 2017 HCN profile (and alternatively test upper-atmosphere temperature inversions).  
  > "We ran a grid of models varying f$_{low}$, z$_{top}$, q$_{top}$, and $\alpha$. f$_{low}$ values of 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2 were tested" (Disk-averaged HCN profile); "Models were compared to data averaged in 9 bins of radial distance, with a bin size of 0.014", i.e. bins at \[0-0.014"\], \[0.014-0.028"\],...,\[0.112-0.126"\]." ## Qualitative findings

- Spatially-resolved CO(3-2) spectra exhibit an absorption core at beam positions within Pluto's disk that vanishes towards the limb, providing direct spectral evidence of Pluto's cold mesosphere (negative temperature gradient above ~30 km).  
  > "The CO line shows an absorption core at beam positions within Pluto’s disk, a direct signature of Pluto’s cold mesosphere." (Abstract); "A new observational result to highlight is the presence of absorption cores for many beam positions, most prominent near disk center and progressively vanishing towards Pluto’s limb." (Temperature / CO retrievals)
- HCN(4-3) emission is much more spatially extended than CO(3-2) and continuum emission, peaking slightly beyond Pluto's limb (~0.065" or ~1.3 Pluto radii).  
  > "On the other hand, the radial distribution of the HCN emission is markedly different, peaking slightly beyond Pluto’s limb." (CO and HCN: data presentation); "in the observations, the HCN emission does not decrease before $\sim$0.065” from disk center, i.e. at $\sim$1.3 Pluto radius." (Initial fitting attempts of the emission radial profiles)
- HCN emission is enhanced above the low-latitude (sky North-East) limb compared to the high-latitude (sky South-West) limb, opposite to the CO emission trend.  
  > "Fig. 2 further shows that the CO emission appears to be enhanced at high Northern latitudes compared to Equatorial latitudes, and the opposite is generally seen for HCN." (CO and HCN: data presentation); "In spite of these difficulties, our observations indicate larger HCN amounts, by a factor of several, above Pluto’s low-latitude vs high-latitude limb." (Search for HCN spatial variations)
- Low HCN abundance in the lower atmosphere and low upper limits on CH3CN and CH3CCH indicate incomplete resublimation of ice-coated haze aerosols in the lower atmosphere.  
  > "These upper limits also point to incomplete resublimation of ice-coated aerosols in the lower atmosphere." (Abstract); "Our new upper limit (5$\times$10$^{-9}$ at 65 km) is $\sim$5 times smaller than the saturated value at 100 K (and $\sim$50 times smaller than saturation at 107 K), implying that resublimation is not complete, and lower than modeled by (cite: lavvas21)." (Mean HCN profile)

## Reasoning

- Why this data: Intermediate ALMA configurations (C40.7, ~0.06" resolution) resolve Pluto's disk (~0.10") and extended atmosphere (~0.15") while retaining sufficient sensitivity for weak atmospheric lines (60-100 mJy), and Pluto's high sub-Earth latitude (~53.5 deg N) allows probing equator-to-pole variations; compact configuration C40.3 (~0.58") maximizes sensitivity for detecting faint HNC while separating Pluto from Charon.  
  > "However, intermediate array configurations of several 10’s of milli-arcseconds (mas) do allow resolution of Pluto’s atmosphere, while providing sufficient sensitivity. A specific advantage is that currently Pluto’s North pole is well positioned for viewing from Earth (sub-Earth latitude on Pluto $\sim$ 50-57$^\circ$ N over 2015-2020) enabling the investigation of equatorial-to-pole variations in the temperature and composition fields." (Introduction); "Because the prime goal of the HNC setup observations was detection, we used a low spatial resolution (ALMA configuration C40.3, with maximum baseline $\sim$460 m and resolution $\sim$0.58$\mbox{$^{\prime\prime}$}$), sufficient to separate Pluto from Charon, while ensuring the entire flux density from Pluto would be measured." (Observations and data reduction)
- Why this method: A constrained and regularized Conrath/Rodgers inversion method is used for CO to retrieve vertical temperature profiles without unphysical oscillations, whereas a parametric forward-modeling grid and single-parameter scaling are used for HCN because the narrow, minimally pressure-broadened HCN lines lack sufficient vertical information content (~1.5 independent layers) and hyperfine satellite lines are not clearly detected in the resolved 2017 data.  
  > "temperature profiles are (i) constrained to remain close to the a priori at levels where the measurements contain no information (ii) regularized, i.e. smoothed to some vertical resolution (usually comparable to the atmospheric scale height) to avoid unphysical oscillations of the solution profiles." (Temperature / CO retrievals); "When fitting the spectra at each beam position, we allowed for a single free parameter, namely a constant scaling factor to this profile. The reason for this decision is the limited information content of the HCN narrow (minimally pressure-broadened) spectra, preventing us from retrieving a HCN vertical profile from lineshape." (Search for HCN spatial variations)
- Alternative: Atmospheric decorrelation correction using check source J1923-2104 (not needed because continuum flux density was sufficient for self-calibration)  
  > "An additional source, J1923-2104, was included as a “check source”. ALMA includes these sources to allow for an atmospheric decorrelation correction, if needed. However, as described below, there was enough flux density to self-calibrate the data so this correction was not necessary." (Observations and data reduction)
- Alternative: Explaining high-latitude CO emission enhancement solely via a latitudinal CO abundance increase toward the North Pole with fixed temperature (disfavored because it yields the smallest chi^2 improvement and contradicts GCM predictions of a North-to-South sublimation flow that transports CO to the southern hemisphere)  
  > "The free CO, fixed temperature, retrievals (second column) would point to somewhat more marked CO variations, but we note that it is the one associated with the smallest “gain" in $\chi^2$ (last line in Fig.6). Furthermore, a CO increase from the Equator to the North Pole is not expected." (Temperature / CO retrievals)
- Alternative: Explaining enhanced low-latitude HCN emission via a ~400 km thick 'hot layer' peaking at 90 K at 800 km altitude (discarded because it is grossly inconsistent with 65-68 K temperatures inferred from New Horizons Alice/REX CH4 profiles, requires ~5 times more thermal conduction than solar EUV/FUV heating can sustain, and produces discrepant profiles at the polar limb)  
  > "However, the thermal profiles returned at these beam positions were found to exhibit a broad ($\sim$400 km FWHM) “hot layer", with peak temperatures of 90 K at 800 km. This i.e. $\sim$20 K higher than the “canonical" 70 K upper atmosphere temperature, and as discussed below grossly inconsistent with inferences from New Horizons / Alice at low latitudes." (Search for HCN spatial variations); "We therefore did not consider this solution any further." (Search for HCN spatial variations)
- How they decided it was enough: _not stated_
- Limitation (authors): The retrieved latitudinal temperature contrast (7 +/- 3.5 K) is only ~2-sigma significant, improvement over the a priori fit (Delta chi^2) is modest, and temperatures retrieved near the polar limb 'beyond' the pole are somewhat higher than near disk center at the same latitudes.  
  > "Within these error bars, the temperature latitude variations are only $\sim$2-$\sigma$ significant" (Temperature / CO retrievals); "While our analysis hints at an elevated stratopause temperature above the summer pole, the comparison of the first two panels of Fig. 8, the maps of $\Delta\chi^2$ (fifth panel of Fig. 6), and visual inspection of Fig. 7, admittedly invites to caution." (Temperature / CO retrievals); "Another note of concern may be the fact that atmospheric temperatures at Northern latitudes (50$^\circ$N-70$^\circ$N) are returned to be somewhat higher from the near-limb position “beyond" the pole than from the near disk-center beam position" (Temperature / CO retrievals)
- Limitation (authors): The 2015 disk-averaged and 2017 disk-resolved datasets do not provide fully consistent results on the HCN vertical distribution, especially if a large lower-atmosphere HCN component is included.  
  > "A general result is that while the increase with altitude of HCN, inferred from the 2015 spectrum shape, is confirmed from the HCN emission radial distribution in the 2017 data, the two datasets do not appear to provide fully consistent results on the HCN vertical distribution." (Disk-averaged HCN profile)
- Limitation (authors): Models of spatial variations in HCN underpredict the observed HCN line areas and contrasts at/beyond the low-latitude limb (reduced chi^2 up to ~1.4), and a satisfactory physical explanation for enhanced low-latitude HCN is lacking.  
  > "in fact the HCN line areas and contrasts at/beyond the low-latitude (sky North-East) limbs are somewhat underpredicted by the model (this can be seen e.g. in Fig. 10, at beam positions near (RA, DEC) = (0.04", 0.04"), and the reduced $\chi^2$ between modelled and observed spectra reaches values of $\sim$1.4" (Search for HCN spatial variations); "In summary, we suggest that the spatial variations of the HCN emission point to enhanced HCN abundances at low vs high latitudes, perhaps combined with some temperature variations in the upper atmosphere, but recognize we are at loss to propose a satisfactory explanation for them." (HCN spatial variations?)
- Caveat (inferred, ours): The spatial resolution (~0.076" x 0.052") only marginally resolves Pluto's ~0.10" solid disk (fewer than ~2-3 independent beams across the disk), so beam smearing strongly couples disk and limb emission.
- Caveat (inferred, ours): Combining three CO/HCN epochs with different sub-Earth longitudes (165-162 deg, 253-249 deg, 85-82 deg) smears out any longitudinal or local-time surface/atmosphere heterogeneity.
