## Qualitative findings

- In the SPHERE/IRDIS H-band image, the top side of the disk surface is clearly visible and separated from the two horn-like features by two dark wedges, whereas in the SPHERE/ZIMPOL I-band image only the two horns are visible and the disk is hidden.  
  > "In the H band image (Fig. 1, middle panel), on the other hand, the top side, [1], of the disk surface is clearly visible, which is consistent with the sub-millimetre observation." (RY Tau in observations); "Two dark wedges separate the disk from the horns, one in the north [5] and one in the south-east [6]." (RY Tau in observations)
- A simple two-component dusty disk wind model with sub-micron to micron-sized grains (<= 1 um) elevated above the disk reproduces both the horn morphology and the shadowing of the disk in the I' band while allowing H-band photons to scatter off the disk.  
  > "A model geometrically reminiscent of a dusty disk wind with small micron to sub-micron-sized grains elevated above the disk can reproduce the optical effect seen in the observations." (abstract)
- A magneto-hydrodynamic (MHD) disk wind is favoured over a photo-evaporative (PE) wind and an infalling protostellar envelope because of the dark wedges separating the horns from the disk, the symmetry of the horns, the small (~10 AU) mm cavity, the presence of inner disk dust, the active jet, and the agreement of the derived mass-loss rate with MHD wind expectations.  
  > "The close to symmetric horn structure in the observations of RY Tau, the opening angle and the presence of the dark wedges between the disk and the horns, all become strong indicators that the material observed above the disk is launched from the disk, rather than material falling onto the disk." (Result & discussion); "We therefore find it more likely that the wind-launching mechanism in this case is magneto-centrifugally driven." (Result & discussion)

## Reasoning

- Why this data: Combining optical (SPHERE/ZIMPOL I band) and near-infrared (SPHERE/IRDIS H band) scattered light polarimetry with sub-millimetre (ALMA Band 6) continuum imaging allows comparison of the disk geometry with the wavelength-dependent shadowing and horn structures above the disk.  
  > "By combining archival observations by SPHERE/ZIMPOL (Zurich IMaging POLarimeter; (cite: Beuzit2019; Schmid2018)), sub-millimetre observations by Atacama Large Millimetre Array (ALMA), and newly acquired near-infrared observations by SPHERE/IRDIS (Infra-Red Dual-band Imager and Spectrograph; (cite: Dohlen2008)), we discuss the possible scattered light detection of the RY Tau disk wind." (Introduction)
- Why this method: Monte Carlo radiative transfer modelling with MCMax3D allows testing whether a simple geometric model of elevated dust grains in a wind can reproduce both the SED and the synthetic scattered light images in I and H bands.  
  > "We used Monte Carlo radiative transfer modelling *MCMax3D* to create comparable synthetic observations that test the effect of a dusty wind on the optical effect in the observations." (abstract)
- Alternative: Infalling protostellar envelope (as proposed by Garufi et al. 2019): disfavoured because the H-band image shows dark wedges separating the disk from the horns and a symmetric horn structure, whereas infalling envelope material would not produce dark wedges and is expected to be asymmetric.  
  > "If the elevated material is remains of the protostellar envelope, lit by the star, one would expect there to be no wedges between the disk and the envelope." (Result & discussion); "The close to symmetric horn structure in the observations of RY Tau, the opening angle and the presence of the dark wedges between the disk and the horns, all become strong indicators that the material observed above the disk is launched from the disk, rather than material falling onto the disk." (Result & discussion)
- Alternative: Photo-evaporative (PE) disk wind: disfavoured because PE models require large inner cleared cavities (20-30 AU) to maximize efficiency and still predict weak scattered light signals below SPHERE/IRDIS noise, whereas RY Tau has a smaller (~10 AU) mm cavity containing inner dust down to 0.21 AU.  
  > "However, the cavity in the disk of RY Tau in millimetre continuum is $\sim 10$ AU, that is to say smaller than in the best case assumed for the PE model. This means that the signal of a PE wind would likely be even lower than predicted by (cite: 2022A&A...657A..69F) in the case of RY Tau." (Result & discussion)
- Alternative: Outflow cavity carved by the jet in a remnant envelope: considered as an alternative that cannot yet be completely ruled out without spatially resolved gas-velocity data.  
  > "While the disk wind hypothesis is intriguing, our analysis does not yet rule out the possibility that the optically observed morphology around RY Tau comes from an outflow cavity as a result of the jet." (Result & discussion)
- How they decided it was enough: _not stated_
- Limitation (authors): The Gaia EDR3 parallax quality is not optimal due to surrounding nebulosity, so the Hipparcos distance (133 pc) is used instead.  
  > "The quality of the parallax, however, is not optimal due to surrounding associated nebulosity." (RY Tau, stellar properties)
- Limitation (authors): The disk scale height could not be estimated directly from the observations and was fixed based on literature radiative transfer modelling.  
  > "Radiative-transfer modelling from (cite: 2013ApJ...772..145T) shows that a scale height of 5AU at a radius of 50AU matches their observations and we, not being able to estimate the scale height from our observations, chose a similar scale height and a modest flaring $\beta=1.14$." (Disks properties)
- Limitation (authors): In the radiative transfer model, the 10 micron silicate emission feature is weaker and less strongly peaked than in the Spitzer observations, and is dominated by the wind rather than the inner disk.  
  > "In our model, however, the silicate feature is weaker than that of the observations by *SPITZER* and not as strongly peaked." (Result & discussion)
- Limitation (authors): The synthetic H-band model images contain numerical noise spots caused by low photon statistics from stray photons scattering in the extended outer disk region.  
  > "The final models contain some numerical noise in the H band images coming from stray photons that make it through the disk and scatter in the introduced outer disk region (see Fig. 4). The appearance of these spots is due to low photon statistics in this region." (Result & discussion)
- Limitation (authors): No spatially resolved gas-velocity data are available to definitively rule out an illuminated jet-carved outflow cavity.  
  > "However, no spatially resolved gas-velocity data available for RY Tau show any similar structures. While the disk wind hypothesis is intriguing, our analysis does not yet rule out the possibility that the optically observed morphology around RY Tau comes from an outflow cavity as a result of the jet." (Result & discussion)
- Caveat (inferred, ours): The disk wind model is purely geometric and ad hoc rather than self-consistently hydrodynamical, and the exact cone angles for the wind foot and wind top are not tabulated in the parameter table.
- Caveat (inferred, ours): The gas-to-dust ratio in the wind is assumed to be 100; if grain growth and settling reduce dust entrainment, the actual gas mass-loss rate could be much higher.
- Caveat (inferred, ours): The 10 micron silicate feature in the model does not match the strength and sharp peak of the Spitzer spectrum.
